Every model release, the same two headlines run side by side: **"It's scarily capable"** and **"Is it conscious yet?"**

The second question feels natural. It carries an assumption nobody checks: *the smarter the machine, the closer it is to having a mind.*

That assumption is wrong — and not newly wrong. It is a **bookkeeping error the whole species has been making for 76 years**. This post explains how the books went wrong, why nobody caught it, and which popular judgments flip once you correct the entry.

## 1. A word that did the quiet work

In English, *robot* comes from the Czech *robota* — **forced labor**. The word's root contains work, not personhood. When English wants a human-shaped machine it reaches for a different word entirely: *android*, *humanoid*. The connection between robots and humans in English is optional, bolted on by a century of science fiction.

Mandarin Chinese has no such firewall. The word is 机器人 — **machine-person** — and the "person" is welded into the root. You cannot say the word without saying "human." A 2026 note in *AI & Society* (Y. Ding, Cambridge) analyzes exactly this: where English's anthropomorphism is narrative and optional, Chinese speaker's is **grammatical and mandatory**.

That makes it the perfect cold open for this post, because the topic is precisely this: **the language we use for machines has been fusing "machine intelligence" with "machine personhood" since before there were any machines worth fusing.**

These are two different things. Let's unwind them.

## 2. Turing did the split in 1950 — himself

The founding paper of the field, Turing's 1950 *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*, is remembered for the imitation game. The more consequential move is a paragraph fewer people quote.

An objection had been raised: for a machine to think, it must first **feel** — the argument from consciousness. Turing's reply contains the most important boundary ever drawn in this discipline:

> "I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about consciousness. … But I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be solved before we can answer the question with which we are concerned in this paper."

Plain translation: **the consciousness question is out of scope for the engineering question.** Five years later, the Dartmouth proposal — the founding charter of "artificial intelligence" — chose the verb *simulate*: make machines exhibit intelligent behavior. Not give them minds.

So the two goals each have a clean pedigree:

**The engineering goal of AI** — success means tasks performed: exams passed, problems solved, code generated. Its criteria are public, dense, and consensual. Benchmarks are its scoreboard.

**The realization goal of the machine heart** — success means a mind actually instantiated: the system doesn't just act as if it has a mind, it *has* one. There is no consensus criterion for this. The most formalized candidate theory (integrated information theory) holds that a purely feed-forward system has zero integration no matter how capable — and that theory itself carries a 124-signatory open letter branding it pseudoscience. One goal has a scoreboard; the other has an argument about whether a scoreboard is possible.

Two goals. Separate journals, separate societies, incommensurable criteria. **The problem: public discourse has never kept them in separate books.**

## 3. How the books got cooked

The error has a name: the **progress-transfer illusion** — silently booking measured progress on the engineering goal as progress toward the machine heart.

Every capability jump gets recorded by the public, the press, and the marketers as "one step closer to a mind." Note that **no such transfer ever actually occurs** — capability belongs on one ledger, mind on the other, and the two do not reconcile. But the phantom transfer happens daily, maintained by four mechanisms:

1. **Conceptual slippage.** Searle separated "weak AI (tool)" from "strong AI (literally a mind)" in 1980. In reception the two collapsed into one — so evidence for the former automatically became evidence for the latter.
2. **Narrative structure.** A progress story needs a summit. "Machines keep getting better at tasks" has no summit; "machines ascend toward mind" does — and a century of culture has stocked that summit with images.
3. **Commercial incentive.** Mind-aura prices higher than capability. The flagship case: the report that announced "sparks of AGI" in GPT-4 contains — on a full-text scan — **no substantive discussion of consciousness at all**. The transfer was performed by the readers, not the authors. The brand "AGI" works precisely because it lets capability milestones borrow the halo for free.
4. **Linguistic inertia.** See 机器人. The words arrive before the analysis, and once they're in place, the assumptions they smuggled in feel like common sense.

## 4. Correct the entry, and four judgments flip

This is the satisfying part: **one conceptual correction rewrites four everyday judgments at once.**

**① Public over-attribution gets a mechanism.** Psychologists documented the "illusion of AI consciousness" in *Science*. The transfer illusion supplies its structure: the public isn't foolish — people register capability progress *correctly*, then post it to the wrong ledger, because the ledger has one column. The remedy isn't ridicule; it's a second column.

**② Milestone personhood panics lose their fuse.** Every release day re-litigates "should AI get personhood" — from the European Parliament's 2017 "electronic person" resolution (rejected by 150+ experts) to each model launch. But a capability milestone is simply not the kind of event that opens such a proceeding: personhood is an institutional-legal act with its own criteria, never an automatic consequence of benchmark scores. A milestone may trigger *assessment*; it must never trigger *conferral* by default.

**③ "AGI" marketing loses its halo.** The word's premium runs on the silent transfer. Itemize the invoice — are you claiming task performance, or proximity to mind? — and most AGI narrative deflates on the spot into "a very strong tool."

**④ The dismissal gets cut too.** The binding runs both ways: the same fusion that inflates systems into proto-persons licenses the sneer "it's just statistics, it doesn't really understand, so it's worthless." But if consciousness was never required for machine intelligence in the first place — a 2026 *Synthese* paper argues exactly this — then "no mind" is no verdict on a tool's worth. The unbundling cuts hype and dismissal with the same motion.

## 5. Why it survived 76 years

Because of an **asymmetry of evaluability**.

The engineering goal produces a dense, public, constantly rising stream of scores. The realization goal produces, by its nature, an argument about criteria. A goal with a scoreboard and a goal without one cannot stably share a discourse — **the scoreboard colonizes the vacuum**. Capability metrics are always available, always quotable; the mind question has no instrument whose verdict anyone would accept. So the phantom transfer drifted downstream for 76 years: not because nobody doubted it, but because every doubt stopped short of the *goal* level.

The academy, meanwhile, has been converging on the split from every direction: 2022–2026 brought Tononi's group formalizing "functionally equivalent without being phenomenally equivalent," a nineteen-author framework concluding "no current AI systems are conscious," the *Synthese* argument that consciousness is irrelevant to intelligence, and *Communications of the ACM* pleading: "separate what machines do from what we wish them to be." All the ingredients were on the table. What was missing was the declaration that completes the assembly: **the machine heart and artificial intelligence are two different goals, and progress does not transfer.**

## 6. This is not rhetoric

The claim has teeth — it can be refuted. Three findings would break it: proof that consciousness is *necessary* for general intelligence (the goals re-merge); a consensus third-person "machine-mind detector" (the evaluability asymmetry collapses); founding documents revealing that AI was constitutively committed to creating minds (then the binding is inheritance, not error). None holds today: Turing's bracket and Dartmouth's "simulate" both point the other way.

And it has a home. In the human-referential fitting theory of AGI, the three boundary clauses — **fit the functional personality structure, never fit phenomenal states, never confer personhood** — are precisely this unbundling held inside a theory: everything human practice needs from the system is deliverable on the functional ledger, and the phenomenal ledger is not the theory's to write.

Two different goals; progress does not transfer. Next time the "is it conscious yet?" headline cycles through your feed, try the question that's actually worth money: **which ledger was that progress posted to?**
