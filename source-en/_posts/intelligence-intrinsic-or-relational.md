Since large models appeared, one question has been asked more and more often: does intelligence live inside a model's weights? To answer it, start with two claims — both of which sound reasonable. One appears often in engineering discussions: “intelligence is a specifiable, internalizable property” — meaning intelligence can be written down as a specification and installed inside a system. The other comes from the human-referential fitting theory of AGI, a framework proposed by the author of this post (the papers are listed at the end): “intelligence is a relational property, constituted relative to human practice” — intelligence is not inside the system; it is constituted in how the system deals with the world. A proposed reconciliation combines them: intelligence arises in practice, is stored in a medium, and activates in practice.

Whether that reconciliation works depends on a more basic question: **in the definitions of intelligence, how do the widely used ones actually describe it?** This post goes through the main definitions in roughly chronological order, then returns to it.

## 1. What is intelligence? The definitions point at the environment

Xunzi, in “Rectification of Names”: “That by which one knows, insofar as it is in a person, is called knowing; knowledge that has corresponded with things is called wisdom.” The standard here is correspondence: wisdom requires answering to things outside. The Mohist canon breaks the process into finer steps: knowing is first a faculty (“knowing is material capacity”); then “with one's knowing one meets the thing and can portray it — like seeing”; finally “with one's knowing one orders things, and the knowing is manifest — like brightness.” The three steps say the same thing: a knowing capacity becomes knowledge only upon meeting things.

In the Nicomachean Ethics Aristotle asks what function is peculiar to humans, and answers: activity in accordance with reason — “rational animal” is the later summary of this argument. Reason there is not the property of some object; it is a definition laid on the human species. The Sanskrit buddhi (wisdom, awakening) carries the root “to wake,” the same root as Buddha; prajñā names a person who has directly realized how things are. The standard etymology of Latin intelligentia is inter-legere, picking out and discerning between — and the one discerning is again a person.

Zhu Xi wrote a sentence in his Mengzi commentary that rewards slow reading: “The mind is the numinous awareness of a person, that by which one holds all principles within and responds to the ten thousand affairs.” The sentence has two halves: “holding all principles within” says the mind already contains the principles of things; “responding to the ten thousand affairs” says the mind must be able to answer them. What is stored inside, and what is done out there — each takes half. He also supplied the missing text on “investigating things and extending knowledge” in the Great Learning: “go to things and exhaust their principles,” and after long effort “suddenly one penetrates through in full comprehension.” Readers working on large models will recognize this narrative; the field calls it grokking. Wang Yangming pushed the standard one step further: “knowing is the beginning of action; action is the completion of knowing”; “to know and yet not act is simply not yet to know” — for him, knowing without doing is not yet knowing. Whether one knows is judged by action (the knowing he discussed was mainly liangzhi, moral knowing, and still a human affair).

These definitions differ in wording and agree in direction: **to speak of intelligence was to speak of how it answers to things and handles affairs; none of them describes it as a stock stored inside a body.** They also share another feature: they are all about human intelligence. Xunzi's subject is “in a person”; Zhu Xi's mind is “the mind of a person.” Intelligence as a concept that does not presuppose its carrier did not exist yet.

## 2. Intelligence became a number, and the definitions grew no clearer

Another set of definitions comes from testing. In 1869 Galton published Hereditary Genius, the first systematic study of the heredity of ability; in 1890 Cattell coined the term “mental tests”; in 1904 Spearman proposed the g factor, one common factor behind mental abilities; in 1905 Binet and Simon built the first intelligence scale; in 1916 Terman turned it into the Stanford-Binet, and the IQ spread across America; in 1939 and 1944, between the two editions of his scales, Wechsler left that definition — intelligence as the individual's global capacity “to act purposefully, to think rationally, and to deal effectively with his environment.” The sentence again splits in two halves: the first ties intelligence to the individual; the second points at the environment.

This era also produced a new development: intelligence and the test began to define each other — tests drafted by the definition, intelligence valued by the score. In 1923 Boring made the circle explicit: “intelligence is what the tests test”; his article is titled “Intelligence as the tests test it.”

The definitions themselves did not converge. In 1921 the Journal of Educational Psychology put “intelligence and its measurement” to a panel of experts, and there were almost as many definitions as respondents; in 1986 Sternberg and Detterman ran the same exercise again with some twenty researchers, one chapter each, with a similar conclusion; Legg and Hutter later compiled some seventy informal definitions, and over a century the list only grew. Outside the argument, however, one direction in psychology became gradually clearer: Sternberg's own 1984 definition is a relational sentence — intelligence as “adaptation to, selection and shaping of, real-world environments”; Piaget put it more directly, “intelligence is adaptation” — assimilation and accommodation, knowing as the product of a person interacting with the environment; Vygotsky widened the relation to society and language; Gardner's multiple intelligences (1983) set no single yardstick. These theories were still about people: children, students, patients.

## 3. The machines arrive, and definitions no longer assume a person

Turing's approach in 1950 was to set the definition aside: not “can machines think,” but the imitation game — the criterion placed inside the conversation between humans and a machine, judged by people. The Dartmouth proposal of 1956 did not define intelligence; it defined manufacturing: “every aspect of learning, or any other feature of intelligence, can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.”

A definition that does not assume a person came from Legg and Hutter in 2007: “Intelligence measures an agent's ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments.” The subject is an agent, not a person; their formal definition writes the sentence as mathematics — expected performance over a distribution of environments. Chollet in 2019 described intelligence as “skill-acquisition efficiency” and built ARC, a test where humans and machines can sit side by side. Tegmark in 2017 kept it shortest: “intelligence is simply the ability to accomplish complex goals.” The goals are outside the system.

China's Cihai dictionary keeps the earlier direction: “intelligence is a person's capacity to know objective things and apply knowledge to solve practical problems” — the subject is a person. China's 2018 White Paper on AI Standardization defines artificial intelligence as “simulating, extending, and expanding human intelligence” — the anchor of machine intelligence returns to human intelligence.

## 4. Where the “inside” view comes from

The “inside” claim has sources of its own — three of them, different in kind.

Plato's Meno makes learning recollection — knowledge already in the soul; Descartes' Meditations establishes “a thing that thinks” — thought as the essence of mind. This is the metaphysical form of the intrinsic view: the mind as a self-sufficient entity.

Galton treated genius as a question of inherited endowment; Jensen's The g Factor (1998) holds that g reflects a biological property of the brain — his later paper is titled The g factor: psychometrics and biology; Dweck gave the folk belief that intelligence is fixed and unchangeable a name — the entity theory — opposed to the growth view. Two points are worth noting about this branch. First, it is about human intelligence — Descartes' mind is a human mind, Jensen's brain a human brain. Second, even if one accepts the position, testing it still returns to behavior — however sure one is that g is in the brain, measuring it means giving people tasks; the circle Boring described is still there.

The engineering version is Hawkins' On Intelligence (2004), which defines the brain as a memory-prediction system. Mechanistic interpretability has carried the discussion into the weights, with the strength of the claims rising in steps: in 2020 Olah and colleagues proposed “features are the fundamental unit of neural networks” — the original text itself marks this as a conjecture; in 2022 Olsson and colleagues found “induction heads” are the main source of in-context learning in small models, with the abstract saying “might constitute the mechanism”; in 2023 Nanda and colleagues “fully reverse engineer the algorithm learned by these networks” on toy tasks; by 2025 the official blog is saying “we found that Claude plans ahead.” A 2023 paper is titled Sparks of AGI, subtitled early experiments with GPT-4. This line of work has also moved on: in larger models, function-vector heads rather than induction heads do the work (Yin et al., 2025); sparse autoencoders find no canonical units of analysis (Leask et al., 2025). Many of the early strong claims were later revised by research in the same field.

Placed together, the claims that “intelligence is inside” turn out to be two different kinds. One is a substantive claim: mind as an entity, g as a brain property, the brain as a prediction machine. The position is a serious one, but with two limits: it is mostly about human intelligence — Descartes' mind is a human mind, Jensen's brain a human brain; and its testing remains relational — believing g is in the brain still means verifying it through people's performance on tasks. The other is engineering phrasing: saying “we found the mechanism” as if it meant “the capacity lives here.” Mechanism attribution is a hypothesis; capacity location is a conclusion; moving from one to the other requires additional conditions. The real tension in the definitions is therefore not between definitions saying relational and definitions saying intrinsic — the definitions have always been relational. The tension is between the definitions and the engineering phrasing.

## 5. A more accurate formulation: intelligence is a relational property, constituted relative to practice

The question left open at the start can now be answered. “Intelligence is a relational property” is not a new idea — Xunzi's correspondence, Wang Yangming's “to know and yet not act,” and Piaget's adaptation all came before; this post places the claim back into that history in order to ask what else it needs.

But for understanding intelligence, calling it relational is not enough; three things need to be added. First, it names the referent: a relation always has a relative-to, and the human-referential theory's answer is the open set of human practice — not the environment in general, and not the human species. Second, it sorts the confusion: intelligence is a capacity concept, human intelligence a sample concept, artificial intelligence an artifact concept, AGI a reference-frame concept — four kinds of concept, each with its own meaning of “definition completed”; and the two kinds of talk — placing intelligence somewhere, taking a score for the definition — are registered in Four Concepts, Four Types as the locational form and promotion. Third, it returns the criteria to the practice side: what should be fitted is practice, not works — in the theory's words, the stock has a wall, the flow does not; practice moves forward every day while models update on a cycle, and that gap has a name, the fitting lag.

## 6. Weights and intelligence: endowment is not intelligence

The opening sentence can now be stated more precisely. The original version says intelligence arises in practice, is stored in a medium, and activates in practice; the difficulty sits in “stored” — it turns intelligence into stock in a warehouse. Written again with a different subject: **intelligence arises in practice; its realization conditions are carried by the realizer; its constitution and its criteria remain in practice.** Finding the mechanism that produces intelligence is not finding intelligence a residence; what can be written down as a specification is the condition, and the condition pays out only upon meeting the environment it was made for.

“A human needs years of practice for intelligence, yet model weights are born carrying it” — with the birth story completed, this reverses. The four terms line up in one table:

| | Human | Model |
|---|---|---|
| What birth/release brings | Instinct — endowment | Dispositions in the weights — endowment too |
| Where the endowment comes from | Practice at the scale of species evolution | Recorded, frozen human practice (training itself is fitting practice) |
| How it becomes capacity | Growing up in the practices of family, education, and society | Running in the practices of real use, tasks, and feedback |
| How it is judged | Performance in life and work, and later psychometric measurement | Performance in carrying out tasks, counterfactual questions, whether updates keep up |

The two rows share one logic: endowment is not intelligence; endowment becomes capacity only upon meeting practice; and capacity is an attribution made jointly with the environment — not stock kept in a body or in weights. The difference is in the source of the endowment: the human's comes from practice at evolutionary scale, the model's from recorded, frozen practice. The model row has more concrete checks: weights are trained out of frozen text, and what is stored there is the statistical regularity of the text — the branches of practice (what if another path had been taken) were already absent when the text took shape. Practice moves forward daily, models update on a cycle; the same weights moved to a different setting of use receive a different verdict. Wang Yangming's sentence from five hundred years ago fits here: to know and yet not act is simply not yet to know — a system that has read every swimming manual without ever entering the water cannot yet be said to know swimming.

**Intelligence is not waiting inside the system to be found; it is an attribution relative to practice — the capacity a system shows in dealing with its environment. The weights hold only endowment: endowment becomes capacity through practice, and the place to judge it has always been the practice side.**
